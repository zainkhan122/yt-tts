In a Virginia bankruptcy court, in September twenty seventeen, the chief executive of Toys R Us put this on paper. The company's overleveraged capital structure, he wrote, had constrained it from making the expenditures that would have fixed the stores. Forbes printed the sentence from his declaration. He also named the cash. About four hundred million dollars a year, just to service the debt.

That is why this film exists. Not a theory about tablets. Not a eulogy for childhood. Four hundred million dollars a year leaving the building, while the shelves were still full of toys.

Keep Brandon's sentence. The twelve years that wrote it are the rest of the record.

March seventeenth, two thousand five. Bain Capital, Kohlberg Kravis Roberts, and Vornado Realty Trust announced they would buy Toys R Us for six point six billion dollars. On July twenty-first, two thousand five, the public stock closed for the last time, at twenty-six dollars and seventy-five cents. Wikipedia logs the date. Forbes, reading the deal, put more than five point three billion of the purchase price on borrowed money. The three firms and their co-investors put in about one point three billion of equity. The stores, the inventory, the name — those became collateral.

A leveraged buyout is not a riddle. You buy a company with the company's own ability to borrow. Then the company pays the interest. If the company is a toy chain, the toys pay the loan.

Charles Lazarus had opened a baby-furniture shop in Washington, D.C., in April nineteen forty-eight. In June nineteen fifty-seven he opened the first Toys R Us, in Rockville, Maryland, and pointed the shelves at toys. That origin is real. It is not why the American stores went dark. The origin that matters is the bill from two thousand five.

Wayne, New Jersey, was headquarters. When the filing came, the company still ran on the order of sixteen hundred Toys R Us and Babies R Us stores around the world. Reuters put the workforce around sixty-four thousand. This was not a boutique that missed a trend. It was a chain that still had customers in the aisles in two thousand five, and in two thousand seventeen.

The owners did try to get out. In May two thousand ten Toys R Us announced plans for a public offering. In March two thousand thirteen it cancelled them, citing market conditions. Wikipedia logs both dates. The equity did not get its door. The interest line stayed.

Here is the bill, in cash, every year.

After the buyout, roughly four hundred million dollars a year went to service the debt. Brandon said so in the declaration. Retail Dive quoted him further: that number made it impossible to keep up on the condition of the stores, on faster shipping, on a subscription delivery service — the things Amazon and Walmart were already building. Forbes put capital spending under the new owners around two hundred fifty million dollars a year. The interest line was larger than the rebuild line. You do not outrun a warehouse network when the first check every year is to the lenders.

Forbes also read the filings on fees. Toys R Us recorded a hundred eighty-three million dollars in advisory fees to the three owners over the life of the deal. The magazine said most of that appears to have been paid. Forty-seven million dollars in transaction fees were later waived, the company said in its S.E.C. papers. A fee is not the whole death. It is where cash went instead of a floor, or a site.

Amazon was not imaginary. Walmart was not imaginary. Target was not imaginary. Discounters were already using toys as a cheap door into the store before the buyout. In two thousand, years before the three firms arrived, Toys R Us had signed Amazon to run its online shop under a ten-year exclusive. They later sued. In two thousand six a court ended the deal. That chapter is real. It is not this film's villain. By the time they got the website back, the balance sheet already owed the two thousand five loan. Brandon, under oath, did not blame a tablet. He blamed an overleveraged capital structure.

The company had not posted an annual profit since two thousand thirteen. Wikipedia logs that. In the quarter ending April twenty-ninth, twenty seventeen, it reported a net loss of a hundred sixty-four million dollars. Forty percent of the year's sales sat in the holiday quarter. CNBC, the day of the filing, put the debt around four point nine billion dollars: four hundred million coming due in twenty eighteen, one point seven billion in twenty nineteen. Vendors had started to demand cash on delivery. Brandon's papers called the rumor a dangerous game of dominos. Forbes wrote that nearly forty percent of product vendors had stopped shipping on ordinary terms.

On September eighteenth, twenty seventeen, Toys R Us Incorporated filed for Chapter eleven in Richmond, Virginia. The company said the case would let it deal with about five billion dollars of long-term debt, and that it had a commitment for more than three billion dollars in debtor-in-possession financing, from a syndicate led by J.P. Morgan. Stores would stay open. Canada would file in parallel. Operations outside the United States and Canada were not in the case. Brandon's press line, on CNBC, was that this was the dawn of a new era, and that the financial constraints would finally be addressed.

Chapter eleven is not a funeral. It is a request to keep the lights on while you recut the debt. Reuters wrote that the court let them borrow more than two billion dollars so suppliers would ship Lego and Barbie into the holiday. The funeral arrived when that holiday did not.

They filed on the doorstep of the quarter that had to feed the year. Suppliers had already tightened. Retail Dive, a year later, wrote that holiday sales fell more than ten percent, and that the company missed the sales and profit targets tied to the bankruptcy loan. That miss was a default. Lenders extended into March. Then they chose the inventory instead of the going concern.

In January twenty eighteen the plan was to close about a hundred eighty United States stores. On March fifteenth, twenty eighteen, the company asked the court to wind down the American business and liquidate the inventory in all seven hundred thirty-five United States stores, including Puerto Rico. About thirty-three thousand people were on that payroll. Brandon said he was disappointed, and that they no longer had the financial support to continue United States operations. Liquidation sales started March twenty-third. The website went to a closing notice on March twenty-ninth.

On June twenty-ninth, twenty eighteen, the remaining United States stores closed. Wikipedia logs the date. Seventy years after Lazarus opened the furniture shop. The Canadian chain — eighty-two stores; the company had said they were profitable — was sold that April to Fairfax Financial for about two hundred thirty-four million dollars, and kept the name. A toymaker tried to raise money to save hundreds of American floors. It did not hold. The trademark later showed up on other people's floors. That is a name living on. It is not seven hundred thirty-five warehouses.

Put Brandon's sentence next to those empty aisles.

Toys R Us did not die because children grew up. Children were still in the market. Amazon was selling them toys. Walmart was selling them toys. What died was a specialty chain whose cash, for twelve years, had a first call from a two thousand five buyout. Four hundred million a year to service debt. Stores Brandon himself said they could not revitalize. A site that could not match a warehouse that did not carry that bill. When the bankruptcy loan's holiday targets were missed, the lenders took the inventory.

That is the mechanism. Not a vanished childhood. A debt-service line.

The last American stores went dark in June twenty eighteen. The toys did not. They checked out of a different aisle — one that did not owe the two thousand five loan four hundred million dollars a year. That is the public record.
