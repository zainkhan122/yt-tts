On October eighteenth, twenty twenty-three, Convoy cancelled every shipment on its marketplace. FreightWaves confirmed the load board was blank. Shippers were told they could take the truck themselves. The company said more details in a day or two.

The next morning, in Seattle, Dan Lewis told the staff it was their last day.

Eighteen months earlier, in April twenty twenty-two, investors had priced Convoy at three point eight billion dollars. That is the gap this film is about. Not a mystery. A weather system that was treated like a climate.

Keep the blank board. We will walk the seven years that wrote it.

Dan Lewis and Grant Goodale were former Amazon people. They founded Convoy in Seattle in twenty fifteen: software to match a shipper who had freight with a carrier who had a truck. Press called it Uber for trucks. Bloomberg, in twenty sixteen, had them landing Unilever as their biggest deal to that point. The poster is real. It is not the mechanism.

In twenty seventeen they raised sixty-two million dollars. Y Combinator's Continuity Fund led. Bill Gates's Cascade and Jeff Bezos's expedition fund were already in the room, with Greylock and Marc Benioff and a list of other names Wikipedia logs. Forbes headlined it as money to fend off Uber itself. GeekWire named them Startup of the Year. In twenty eighteen CapitalG led a hundred eighty-five million at a billion-dollar valuation. In November twenty nineteen, Generation and T. Rowe Price led four hundred million at two point seven five billion. Uber Freight was in the same weather. So were the old phones at C.H. Robinson. The poster was: the app wins the spread.

That is a brokerage with a software story, priced like a platform.

A freight brokerage lives in the spread: what the shipper pays, minus what the truck is paid, minus the people who make the match. When trucks are scarce, the match is precious. When trucks are everywhere, the match is a commodity. Convoy bet that code would keep the match precious anyway.

Then the country needed goods moved in a hurry. Truckload rates ran hot. In April twenty twenty-two Convoy raised two hundred sixty million dollars at three point eight billion dollars. FreightWaves: Baillie Gifford and T. Rowe Price among the equity; a hundred million of that round was venture debt from Hercules. GeekWire later noted a hundred fifty million line from J.P. Morgan. The company said it expected to pass a billion dollars of revenue that year.

That is the spike, booked as a company. A billion dollars of revenue is a brokerage the size of a weather system. It is not proof the weather stays.

Here is the bill, without a chart. You hire toward the hot rate. Peak headcount, GeekWire later put at about fifteen hundred people. You chase Fortune five hundred full-truckload accounts. Flexport's Ryan Petersen, after the funeral, wrote that with that scale came complexity and burn, and that even with the tech they did not reach the scale that turns a profit. That is his line, not ours. The public record of Convoy's own cuts is simpler: the raise was April. The first layoff was June.

June twenty twenty-two, about seven percent, months after the three-point-eight number. October twenty twenty-two, more. February twenty twenty-three, Atlanta closed. June twenty twenty-three, another round, and Goodale stepped off the floor into an advisory role. By the end they were about five hundred people. Lewis told GeekWire that summer they had become even more of a tech company as they trimmed. A former executive told the same paper the tech advantage was not wide enough. Other brokers had apps, or built them.

Would a three-point-eight-billion-dollar match still be a match if any phone could find a cheap truck?

The trucks came back. Demand dipped. Revenue per load fell. Analysts GeekWire quoted in October twenty twenty-three said shippers could get rates from big asset carriers, and some pulled freight onto their own fleets. Craig Fuller of FreightWaves called it one of the deepest freight recessions in decades, plus a change in investor appetite from risk to unit economics. Lewis, in the memo, named the same storm: a massive freight recession, and a contraction in the capital markets. He said they were running up the down escalator, and it kept speeding up. He said the logical buyers were getting crushed too.

August twenty twenty-three, press said they had hired a bank and were looking at a sale. No deal arrived that was enough. FreightWaves checked the docket on October eighteenth: no bankruptcy filing. The board went blank anyway.

On October nineteenth Lewis wrote that they had spent four months on every option, and none of it was enough to keep the core going. He thanked the people who had finished the in-transit loads so the freight already rolling would still arrive. Then he said today was the last day for everyone who had not already been asked to stay for a windup. GeekWire reported laid-off staff did not get severance. Wikipedia, citing the press, says remaining staff were told the options were worthless. More than five hundred people were out. Lewis signed the memo with the old hashtag.

He also wrote that the tech-centric approach had created real benefits, and the conditions for a platform that would have paid when the market improved. He said market forces were too strong to withstand alone. That is the founder, on the record: a recession in the thing they sold, plus a door that closed on unprofitable late-stage companies.

That is the death of the broker. Not a courtroom. A cancelled load.

Two weeks later Flexport bought the technology stack, not the company, not the liabilities. Petersen said the price relative to value was modest. FreightWaves later wrote that the figure reported at the time was sixteen million dollars, and that neither side confirmed it. We will not pretend an unconfirmed price is an audit. We will say this: the thing investors had called three point eight billion was not what sold. A small group of product and engineering people went with the code. Lewis went too, for a while. Flexport later relaunched a Convoy Platform as software for other brokers. In twenty twenty-five DAT bought that platform. Those are other companies. This film ends when Convoy Inc. cancelled the loads.

Put the blank board next to April twenty twenty-two.

Convoy did not die because America stopped shipping. Trucks were still rolling. It died because a digital brokerage had been sized for a rate that was a spike, then asked late-stage money to fund the climb down. When the money wanted unit economics, and the trucks were plentiful, the match was no longer a company. It was a cost.

The last marketplace went dark in October twenty twenty-three. Freight kept moving. It just did not need a three-point-eight-billion-dollar middle to move. That is the public record.
