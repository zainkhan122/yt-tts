Katerra built the wall in a factory, with the windows already in it. That was the product. A truck would roll onto an apartment site, and a finished wall would come off the bed. Architect, mill, contractor — Katerra said those were handoffs. The company in Menlo Park would do all of it. No handoff.

On June first, twenty twenty-one, The Information reported that Katerra had told employees it planned to shut down. More than two billion dollars had gone in, the paper said, to slash the cost of building apartments. Thousands of people. Dozens of jobs the company was likely to walk away from. Five days later, Katerra filed in Houston.

Michael Marks co-founded Katerra in twenty fifteen. He had run Flextronics, the factory that built other companies' electronics. He had been interim chief executive at Tesla. The other founder, Fritz Wolff, came out of real estate. In January twenty eighteen Marks sat with CNBC and described the machine. We have our own factories. We make sub-assemblies. We bring them to a site. We bring the buildings to life. There's no handoff. We do everything.

That same week, SoftBank's Vision Fund led eight hundred sixty-five million dollars into Katerra. CNBC put the valuation just over three billion dollars. Forbes said total money raised had reached one point one billion. Marks told CNBC the company had thirteen hundred people, including a hundred architects, and one point three billion dollars of work waiting to go in the ground. Forbes, looking at the end of twenty seventeen, counted eleven jobs underway and thirty-three in the backlog.

They did not stop at walls. In twenty eighteen Katerra bought Michael Green Architecture in Vancouver, a firm known for tall wood. Global Construction Review said Katerra already sold architecture and interiors, and had bought a Spokane architecture studio the year before. In September twenty nineteen it bought two general contractors — one in Arizona, one in Georgia — and said both would take the Katerra name. The company told the Review those deals added three hundred twenty people.

The factories grew with the stack. Phoenix opened in twenty seventeen: a quarter million square feet. Spokane's mass-timber plant — two hundred seventy thousand square feet — opened in twenty nineteen. Tracy, California, was larger. Five hundred seventy-seven thousand square feet. Dozens of robots on the line. Construction Dive, in twenty eighteen, reported a Spokane mill sized to turn out millions of cubic feet of cross-laminated timber a year.

The pitch to a developer was one sentence. You bring the land. We design it, we engineer it, we build it. Forbes quoted Marks: a fully integrated, turn-key supplier. Because Katerra owned the architect and the factory and the site, a cost overrun had nowhere to go — except onto Katerra.

Cameron Lamming found that out in San Diego. The Information, on October thirty-first, twenty nineteen, reported that Lamming, at the developer RAR Hospitality, had hired Katerra to design and build a hotel. Katerra had just raised nearly a billion dollars from SoftBank. It estimated half the time, and five percent less. Lamming said: given how much money they raised, I wanted to make this bet on them.

That spring, Katerra pulled out. Costs were rising. The timeline was dragging. Lamming was left looking for a new contractor. He told the paper: it's a good pitch. They didn't deliver on any of it. It was a nightmare.

The same reporting, as Global Construction Review later summarized it, said former employees and customers told The Information that Katerra had withdrawn from at least six apartment and hotel projects in the United States over higher-than-expected costs, and that more than a hundred people had already been laid off. A Katerra spokesperson told the paper it is common for construction deals to pause or stop, and called the changes a sign of a healthy, evolving firm.

In December twenty nineteen the Phoenix factory was scheduled to close. Two hundred jobs. The company called Phoenix a first-generation plant and pointed at Tracy's robots. Bloomberg, as Archinect repeated it, said those Phoenix workers would get a ninety-day severance. The last week of the company would not look like that.

Money kept arriving. In January twenty nineteen The Information reported that SoftBank planned another seven hundred million dollars, which would put Katerra's value over four billion and its total equity past two billion. The paper said that might make Katerra one of the Vision Fund's biggest bets in the United States, behind WeWork and Uber. Same checkbook. Different machine. This is not a WeWork film.

By May twenty twenty, Marks was out as chief executive. Paul Kibsgaard, who had run Schlumberger, took the chair. Global Construction Review said about four hundred people were laid off around then — about seven percent of payroll. At the end of twenty twenty, the Review, citing the Wall Street Journal, reported SoftBank was putting in another two hundred million dollars and taking a majority stake. Greensill Capital, a lender partly owned by SoftBank, would take five percent of Katerra in exchange for forgiving four hundred thirty-five million dollars of debt. Kibsgaard told shareholders the company needed that help to continue as a going concern.

Greensill went into insolvency in March twenty twenty-one. On June sixth, Katerra's own release named three reasons for the drop: the pandemic's effect on construction, an inability to get bonding after its former lender's insolvency, and failed attempts to raise more capital and more work. That is Katerra's line. American construction sites were not uniformly dark in twenty twenty-one. We are not here to bless the company's weather report.

The June sixth release is specific. Katerra and certain United States and Cayman entities filed under Chapter eleven in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas, in Houston. SoftBank's investment advisers committed thirty-five million dollars to fund operations in court. International operations, the company said, were not part of the filing. Many United States projects would be demobilizing. An architecture studio and a renovations business were lined up for sale.

The factories did not vanish with the name. On August fourth, twenty twenty-one, Volumetric Building Companies announced that the Houston court had approved its purchase of the Tracy plant. Five hundred seventy-seven thousand square feet. Wall panels, floors, trusses, windows, cabinets. The buyer said construction demand was robust, and it wanted former Katerra staff back on the line. Global Construction Review reported the Spokane mill — one of the largest cross-laminated timber presses in the world — went to a Mercer subsidiary. The Review put that mill at about fifty million dollars when it was built.

So the robots could still make a wall. The press could still make a timber panel. What died was the company that needed to own every step before any of those machines paid for themselves.

A plant like Tracy only gets cheap when it stays full. Housing does not arrive in a smooth ribbon. It arrives as a parcel of land, as a city permit, as a weather window, as a local trade that still has to stand that wall on a foundation the mill did not pour. When Katerra was the architect and the mill and the contractor, a delay was not a subcontractor's problem. It was a line that sat idle, and a contract Katerra had to eat.

They built the stack so there would be no handoff. Then there was no one left to hand the loss to.

The Tracy floor is still a factory. The Spokane press still exists. Katerra does not. The jobsite was never going to disappear just because a wall showed up with the windows already in it.
