Circuit City's six hundred twenty-five stores opened late on February fifth, two thousand three. The company had called the floor in before the doors. Stereophile reported the number the same week: about three thousand nine hundred commissioned salespeople, dismissed. Ten repair centers, shut. The chief executive, Alan McCollough, said the move to hourly pay would save about one hundred thirty million dollars in payroll that year.

That is the death, five years early. The filing came later.

Samuel Wurtzel started it in Richmond, Virginia, in nineteen forty-nine, as Wards Company — television sets, then appliances, then a warehouse showroom he called a loading dock. In nineteen seventy-eight the big format took the Circuit City name. In nineteen eighty-four the company listed on the New York Stock Exchange. By two thousand it employed about sixty thousand people. Wikipedia says the chain became known for exceptional service, with many staff factory-trained. That is the thing a warehouse competitor could not copy on day one.

In nineteen eighty-eight, Wikipedia records, Circuit City had a chance to buy a growing rival, Best Buy, for about thirty million dollars. Chief executive Richard Sharp rejected the offer. He thought Circuit City could open in Minneapolis and beat them. Keep that as a chapter. It is not the firing.

In July two thousand the company walked away from large appliances in every store. The year before, it had been the second-largest appliance retailer in the United States, behind only Sears, with nearly one point six billion dollars in appliance sales. Executives feared Home Depot and Lowe's, and wanted the warehouse cost gone. They missed the housing boom that followed. That is a real cut. It is not the one that emptied the floor of people who knew a television.

The stores themselves changed shape. Wikipedia: in two thousand, Circuit City moved toward a more self-serve big box it called Horizon. The red tower entrance, built so drivers in Los Angeles could find the store among a strip of signs, came down. Shopping baskets went in. Central checkout moved to the front. A person who used to walk you to a wall of televisions was already being designed out of the floor plan. February two thousand three finished the job on the payroll.

February fifth, two thousand three. Stereophile: about sixty percent of Circuit City workers were on commission. About twenty-one hundred of the people let go would be replaced by new hires on the hourly system. The dismissals averaged three people per store. Two hundred repair-center workers went with the ten shops. Circuit City had already shrunk service centers from thirty-seven to seventeen in four years. After this, seven. McCollough's math was payroll. One hundred thirty million dollars saved, against an expected forty million dollar hit from severance and one-time charges.

Commission is not a morality play. Some customers hated the push. Some customers came in because a person who had been factory-trained could tell a Sony from a box. Circuit City paid those people a cut of the sale. Then it decided the cut was the problem. Hourly is cheaper. Hourly does not remember your last receiver. A warehouse already sold the box. After February two thousand three, Circuit City started selling the box too, with fewer of the people who had been the reason to park there.

Same-store sales were already soft. Stereophile put December net income at one point seven four billion dollars, down five percent, same-store down six. A recession was the weather. The company answered weather by firing the floor.

Wikipedia later quotes company insiders who called the commission cut the most influential decision on the way to the end. Treat that as what insiders said, not as a court finding. The public calendar is enough. Five years after the late opening, the chain was in court.

On November tenth, two thousand eight, Circuit City filed under Chapter eleven in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Eastern District of Virginia. Reuters reported it the same day. The New York Times did too. Wiki, from the filings: about three point four billion dollars in assets, two point three two billion in debt, including reported sums owed to Hewlett Packard and Samsung. The court allowed a loan of one point one billion dollars to keep operating. The same day, press recorded a plan to close one hundred fifty-five stores. Chief executive James Marcum said the stores that remained would stay open. He said the chain would not be liquidated.

On January sixteenth, two thousand nine, the company said it would liquidate all of its stores. Wikipedia, from the company, puts the jobs lost in that liquidation at more than thirty thousand. On March eighth, two thousand nine, the original company was done. The last sales ran under banners that said the opposite of February fifth, two thousand three. That Wednesday they had opened late to keep a payroll number. This March they did not open at all.

The recession is in every obituary. Best Buy is in every obituary. Both were real. Neither is why six hundred twenty-five stores opened late on a Wednesday in February two thousand three. They opened late because the company had to tell the commissioned floor it was over, so the next paycheck could be one hundred thirty million dollars lighter.

A store that sells a box can live next to another store that sells a box. A store that had sold knowledge, and then fired the knowledge, is just a worse warehouse. Circuit City got the savings. Then it got the warehouse.

The red circle still exists as a mark. The company that staffed it does not. February fifth, two thousand three. Three thousand nine hundred people. One hundred thirty million dollars. November tenth, two thousand eight. January sixteenth, two thousand nine. That is the public record.
