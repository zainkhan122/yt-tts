This is MoviePass, a real company founded in New York in twenty eleven: three million members by June twenty eighteen, then the app went dark.

People did not stop going to the movies. MoviePass died because it sold a ticket for less than a ticket costs, and then it begged the world to buy more.

Stay with that sentence. We will cash it at the middle of this story.

The idea did not start as a suicide pact. Stacy Spikes had worked in film marketing. He has said the spark was New York art-house theaters that let you see a lot of movies for a flat fee. For years the chains said no. In twenty eleven he and Hamet Watt launched MoviePass anyway.

The early version was not nine dollars and ninety five cents. Spikes later described an average of about thirty five dollars a month, and a small base of roughly twenty three thousand people. That is a different animal. A person who pays thirty five dollars might go twice. The company might survive. Theaters hated it anyway. They thought a middleman would steal the window.

For six years MoviePass stayed small, raising venture money, fighting for theater access, living on the hope that data about who sits in which seat would be worth more than the ticket. That hope never had to carry three million people.

Then Helios and Matheson Analytics bought a majority stake, in August twenty seventeen, and changed the only number that mattered. Unlimited movies. One a day. Nine dollars and ninety five cents a month.

If you were alive in twenty seventeen you remember the feeling. A night out that used to cost a full ticket, plus popcorn you still pay for, now cost less than a streaming app. Friends sent the link. Parents signed up. The internet treated it like a glitch in the store.

It was not a glitch. It was a price.

By June twenty eighteen, more than three million people had signed up. Wikipedia and the contemporary press put it there. Three million monthly fees sounds like a rocket. It was a furnace.

Here is the machine, in one still, no chart tricks. A theater sells a seat for something like twelve to fifteen dollars in many U.S. cities in that era. MoviePass paid the theater. The member paid nine ninety five for the whole month. If you went once, MoviePass might break even or lose a little. If you went twice, the company paid two full tickets and collected one cheap subscription. If you went four times, you were not a customer. You were a hole.

Gyms survive because most members do not show up. That is called breakage. MoviePass's new price destroyed breakage. The deal was too good to leave on the table. The more loyal you were, the more you cost.

Stacy Spikes has said, on the record to Business Insider, that he fought the ten dollar price. He said growth was running on the order of a quarter million new members a month by the end of twenty seventeen. He said he and the new leadership just disagreed on the approach. On January ninth, twenty eighteen, he said he got an email that his services were no longer needed.

I am not asking you to pick a hero. I am telling you the cofounder who built the thing warned that the rocket was pointed at the ground, and then he was gone.

Mitch Lowe, a former Netflix executive, and Ted Farnsworth of Helios were the public face of the new era. They talked as if data and scale would save the math. Scale is not a savior when each new user is a loss. Scale is an accelerant.

Would you have cancelled, or would you have gone every day?

Be honest. Most people who signed up did what a rational person does with a too-cheap buffet. They ate.

Summer twenty eighteen is when the public could see the smoke. Helios filed paperwork aimed at raising huge sums to keep MoviePass alive. In late July the company said it had been forced to borrow five million dollars just to continue. That same stretch, around the release of Mission Impossible Fallout, the merchant processor that advanced money for tickets stopped advancing. Wikipedia and Business Insider both record the blackout. Hundreds of thousands of members opened the app and found they could not buy the movie everyone else was talking about.

That is the crack. Not a villain speech. A processor saying no.

When a subscription company cannot buy the thing it promised, trust dies faster than cash. MoviePass started changing the rules. Caps. Blackouts. Peak films restricted. Press at the time described a shift toward a handful of movies a month instead of one a day. A plan that had been sold as unlimited began to feel like a baited hook. Class actions followed. I am not a court. I will not pretend to rule on those suits. I will say this: the product people bought was not the product they kept.

Theater chains felt the heat too. AMC and others rolled their own subscription plans rather than let a middleman price a seat below the seat. That is not a subplot. It is what happens when your only advantage is being cheaper than physics. You teach the incumbent how to copy the poster without copying the hole.

By October twenty eighteen, reports said more than a million people had cancelled. By April twenty nineteen, paying subscribers were about two hundred twenty five thousand. That is more than a ninety percent drop from the peak of three million. Wikipedia logs those figures. The rocket had dumped its fuel over the towns it flew across.

On September fourteenth, twenty nineteen, MoviePass announced it was closing because efforts to recapitalize had not succeeded. Three days later Ted Farnsworth resigned as CEO and chairman. The app went dark.

On January twenty eighth, twenty twenty, Helios and Matheson Analytics filed for Chapter Seven bankruptcy and said it had ceased all business operations. Chapter Seven is not a restructure. It is a funeral. Paste Magazine wrote the obituary. The parent listed liabilities far above remaining assets. Subscribers showed up in the filing as creditors.

Now we cash the sentence from the open.

MoviePass did not die because cinema died. Cinema was still selling popcorn. It died because the unit economics were a suicide pact. They collected a small monthly number and paid a large per-visit number. They then optimized for the one metric that made the hole bigger: members.

That is the villain. Not a mysterious conspiracy. Arithmetic.

You can love the dream. Unlimited movies is a beautiful poster. You can hate the executives. You can feel for Spikes, fired after objecting, later allowed by a New York bankruptcy court in twenty twenty one to buy the name back. That buyback is real. It is not this story's ending. This story ends when the math closed the app.

If a business makes more money every time you use it less, it is a gym. If it loses money every time you use it more, it is MoviePass at nine ninety five. Remember that when the next app sells you the country for the price of lunch.

This was MoviePass. New York, twenty eleven. Three million members by June twenty eighteen. Dark in September twenty nineteen. Chapter Seven in January twenty twenty. Public record.
