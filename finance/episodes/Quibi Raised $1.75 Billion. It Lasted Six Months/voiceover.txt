One point seven five billion dollars into Quibi. Six months of life, then the app went dark. Founded in Los Angeles in twenty eighteen.

People did not stop watching their phones. Quibi died because it charged for ten minutes of Hollywood on a screen that already gave ten minutes away, and because it did not even own the ten minutes.

Stay with that. We cash it when the library sells for pennies.

The idea had a pedigree. Jeffrey Katzenberg had run Disney’s studio and co-founded DreamWorks. In August twenty eighteen, in Los Angeles, he started a company called NewTV. Meg Whitman, who had run eBay and Hewlett Packard, became chief executive. In October they named it Quibi, from quick bites: shows chopped into episodes of ten minutes or less, built for a phone you turn in your hand.

They did not whisper. In twenty eighteen they raised on the order of a billion dollars from the same Hollywood that was busy launching its own streaming apps: Disney, Fox, NBCUniversal, Warner, Sony, Viacom, and names in tech and banking beside them. By the time the thing launched, the public number was one point seven five billion. Late in twenty nineteen they said the first year’s ads were sold out, a hundred and fifty million dollars of inventory. This was not a garage.

The pitch was a commute. You are in line. You are on a train. You will not start a two-hour film. You might start a chapter. Hollywood quality, they said, not a kid in a bedroom. Turn the phone and the picture turns with you. They called the trick Turnstile. At CES in twenty twenty they sold the full launch: content, tech, partners. A subscription: four dollars and ninety nine cents a month with ads, seven ninety nine without. For people who signed up around launch, a ninety-day free trial. TechCrunch had the prices, and a fifty-show opening slate, in March twenty twenty, before the doors opened.

April sixth, twenty twenty, the app went live in the United States and Canada. TechCrunch reported about three hundred thousand downloads on day one, and a number-three spot in the App Store. If you only read that sentence, it looks like a rocket. It was a trial. Trials are not a business.

Here is the machine, without a chart. Quibi paid Hollywood rates for short chapters. Variety later wrote that some hours of it cost up to six million dollars. Wikipedia, citing the Times, puts more than a billion dollars into commissioning shows in the first year. The member was supposed to pay five or eight dollars a month, on a phone, after a long free window. The shows were not a library Quibi owned. Deals left copyright with the creators. Quibi held licenses. That will matter when there is a funeral.

They also built a fence. For months the product was mobile-only. No living-room app. You could not start it on the television the way you started Netflix. Variety later wrote that Katzenberg had treated mobile video as white space. The white space was already occupied. YouTube was on that phone. TikTok was on that phone. Instagram and Snapchat were on that phone. They were free, infinite, and already a habit.

Quibi’s answer was stars and a price. That is a studio’s answer. It is not an answer to a feed.

Would you have paid, if the same glass already had a bottomless free feed?

Be honest. Most people who downloaded the trial did what a rational person does with a free sample next to a free buffet. They tasted. They did not stay. Press later argued about conversion rates. Quibi disputed the firms. We will not pick a percentage from a fight we cannot audit. We will pick the ending everyone agrees on: the app went dark, and the paying crowd at the death notice was about half a million.

April twenty twenty was not a commute. Katzenberg said so, on the record to the New York Times that May: the performance was not close to what they wanted, and he attributed what had gone wrong to coronavirus. We will not pretend a pandemic did not scramble a launch. We also will not let a pandemic be the whole autopsy. Other apps launched into the same year and found a couch. Quibi’s own theory required a life that had just been cancelled, and a screen people were trying to get off of.

They patched. AirPlay and Chromecast in June, so a phone could throw a picture at a TV. Executive pay cuts. Press in June said they were on track for about two million subscribers in year one against a projection of seven point four million. Native apps for Apple TV, Fire TV, and Android TV showed up in October — the same month the board decided to stop. Variety noted those living-room apps arrived about two days before the shutdown plans went public. That is not a strategy. That is a eulogy with a remote.

On October twenty-first, twenty twenty, the Wall Street Journal reported the end. Katzenberg and Whitman confirmed it. Katzenberg told Deadline there was no question that keeping the company going would not change the outcome, it would only spend more of other people’s money. At the announcement, contemporaneous reports put subscribers around five hundred thousand. The last day of service was December first, twenty twenty. Deadline and Variety both logged the lights out. Six months from April sixth. If you count from the name in twenty eighteen, a little over two years of company and half a year of product.

Now we cash the sentence from the open.

Quibi did not die because phones died. Phones were winning. It died because it sold a paid, Hollywood, ten-minute, phone-only object into a feed that already did short video for free, and because the object was rented. In January twenty twenty-one, Roku bought the rights to the library — seventy-five programs — for less than a hundred million dollars, and put them on a free, ad-supported channel. IndieWire and CNET recorded the licenses. One point seven five billion went in. Less than a hundred million came out the other side of the corpse. That is not a mystery. That is what a license is worth when the customers have left.

You can blame the calendar. You can blame the missing TV app. You can feel for the crews who made the chapters. All of that is real. It is not the villain. The villain is a product nobody needed to pay for, built on rights nobody at Quibi got to keep.

If a business charges you for a bite the phone already pours for nothing, it is Quibi. If it does not own the bite, the funeral is a rummage sale. Remember that when the next app sells you the in-between for the price of lunch.

This was Quibi. Los Angeles, twenty eighteen. One point seven five billion. Live in April twenty twenty. Dark in December. Public record.
