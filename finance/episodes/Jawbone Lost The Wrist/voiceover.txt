Jawbone sat out Christmas twenty fourteen. The UP3, the band with the heart-rate monitor, was not in stores. Fortune printed the delay: a production glitch. Hosain Rahman called it a hiccup.

Three summers later the company was in liquidation.

That gap is this film. Not a missing category. A band they could not ship on time, after they had already bet the company on the wrist.

Keep the empty Christmas. We will walk the sixteen years that wrote it.

Alexander Asseily and Hosain Rahman met at Stanford. They founded AliphCom in San Francisco in nineteen ninety-nine. Asseily had drawn a wrist communicator in a thesis. They ended up with noise-suppression work at Lawrence Livermore, and grants from the Navy and DARPA. Fortune logs that origin. The first product was a headset. Wired liked the military trick in two thousand four. The clip version flopped. Rahman later told Fortune that Steve Jobs had savaged the clip. That meeting is real. It is not why the company died in twenty seventeen.

Fortune also logs a near-death before the headset hit. Mayfield, an early investor, threatened to close the doors after the wired clip flopped. Another DARPA grant kept the lights on. Most of the staff left. Rahman called it nuclear winter. That winter is a chapter. The later winter is the band.

The Bluetooth Jawbone headset landed in two thousand six. Fortune: AliphCom was out of cash, the factory would not ship, and early backers extended a line of credit so Cingular — later AT and T — could stock the stores before Christmas. The headset years threw off sizable profits in two thousand seven and two thousand eight. Hands-free driving laws helped. Then cars built the Bluetooth in, and two thousand nine took the sales away. Rahman called an earlier near-death the nuclear winter. Hardware companies collect those winters.

They kept making objects. The Jambox speaker around twenty ten. The Verge later said Jawbone had been the U.S. market leader in Bluetooth speakers. The last new speaker, the same paper said, was twenty thirteen. The UP band arrived in twenty eleven. Yves Béhar's name on the look. Wikipedia logs the dates. The company took the Jawbone name in two thousand eight and made it official in twenty ten. Wellness, Fortune wrote, became the primary thrust as sleep and steps caught on.

The first UP malfunctioned. Fortune: a production snafu, and a full-price refund for anyone who asked. The Verge later put the same apology on the record. Hardware is not a software patch. You cannot push a new wrist overnight.

By January twenty fifteen Fortune had them as a sixteen-year-old unicorn: more than four hundred million dollars raised, last round above three billion dollars, about four hundred fifty people, hundreds of millions in revenue, and still not profitable. Ben Horowitz on the board. Sequoia, Kleiner, Khosla, Silver Lake, BlackRock. Rahman said everything they had done for fifteen years was preparation for what they were about to do. The UP3 was the about-to-do. It had missed Christmas.

Fortune, in that same piece, wrote the mechanism in plain English: execution misses, slow strategic moves, a scramble for cash, and a struggle to ship a quality product on time — before Apple Watch even arrived, with Fitbit already cheaper on the same wrist. Apple Watch is weather. Fitbit is weather. A band that refunds, then misses Christmas, then leaves the factory is the company.

Would you have waited for a prettier band, if a cheaper one was already on the shelf?

Be honest. Most people did not wait.

The Verge, in May twenty sixteen, confirmed Jawbone was trying to sell the audio business. The last headset, early twenty fourteen. Sources told The Verge a clinical-grade heart wearable was the last chance — not another fitness tracker — and that UP production had stopped, leftover bands sold to a third party. The new object was supposed to be announced that summer. It was not a consumer hit you can name. Jawbone would not comment except to say it was not exiting wearables. Recode, via The Verge, put January twenty sixteen's extra hundred sixty-five million on the pile, bringing the running total toward a billion. TechCrunch, at the funeral, cited PitchBook around nine hundred fifty-one million, including a BlackRock-managed check on the order of three hundred million about a year before the end.

June twenty sixteen Rahman blogged that they were still committed. TechCrunch, in twenty seventeen, noted the social accounts had gone quiet and the comment threads were unpaid complaints. Forbes, citing Business Insider, later wrote that the outside customer-service shop was cut after the bills were not paid.

The Fitbit suits are real. A judge, then later federal charges against some Fitbit people over secrets. We are not a court. Those cases are not this death. This death is a band that left the factory late, then left the factory altogether.

In July twenty seventeen TechCrunch confirmed the liquidation. Sherwood Partners to wind it down. Rahman to a new company, Jawbone Health Hub, new investor, clinical pitch, some staff moving over. Bloomberg and the Financial Times logged the same week. The consumer company was done. BBC, a year later, said the leftover trackers came off the shops.

Put Christmas twenty fourteen next to that.

Jawbone did not die because people stopped caring what they wore on a wrist. Fitbit was still selling. Apple was putting a watch there. Jawbone died because it had become a hardware company whose next object would not ship, after it had already spent the unicorn money as if the object were already in the drawer. When the band stopped, the company was a cost.

The last consumer Jawbone went into liquidation in July twenty seventeen. Wrists were still busy. They were just not wearing that band. That is the public record.
