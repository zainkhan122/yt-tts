On January thirty-first, twenty twenty-four, The Messenger died by email. Three hundred people. The website went blank the same day.

It did not die because America stopped reading. It died because it built a two-thousand-ten newsroom to live on twenty twenty-three leftover ads. Fifty million dollars in. About three million dollars of revenue. Eight months.

Hold the email. We come back when the lights are already off.

Jimmy Finkelstein had just sold The Hill. Nexstar bought it in twenty twenty-one. He set up in West Palm Beach, Florida, under JAF Communications, and raised fifty million dollars. Axios named the check-writers: Mark Penn, Victor Ganzi, Josh Harris, James Tisch, Thomas Peterffy. The pitch was down-the-middle news, free, paid for by ads. Events later. A newsroom in New York, Washington, and Los Angeles.

In March twenty twenty-three he told the New York Times the plan: at least a hundred and seventy five journalists at the start. About five hundred fifty in a year. A hundred million unique readers a month. Axios, closer to launch, put the opening newsroom around a hundred and fifty, and a hundred million dollars of revenue by twenty twenty-four.

May fifteenth, twenty twenty-three, the site went live. Wikipedia logs more than a hundred thousand unique daily visitors four days later. That looks like a pulse. It is not a hundred million a month.

That is the whole origin. The rest is the bill.

The names on the masthead were the cost of the poster. Dan Wakeford, formerly of People, as editor in chief. Politics from Politico. Sports from The Athletic. Tech from Gizmodo. Entertainment from Entertainment Weekly. Axios said some of those hires came in above the going rate. You do not pay that to run a blog. You pay that to look like a paper that already won.

Here is the machine, without a chart. You hire prestige names at above-market pay. You lease offices in three cities. Fortune, citing the Associated Press, later wrote that the company signed multimillion-dollar leases in New York, Washington, and Florida. You promise a newsroom the size of a major paper. The product is free. The money is supposed to be advertising — the same pool Google and Meta already drink from, on the same phone, in a year when general news is a commodity. Volume is the strategy. Wikipedia, citing staffers, later said a lot of the site read like aggregation: other people's stories, rewritten for speed.

A free site that size is a bet that traffic will show up, and that ads will pay for the people. In two thousand ten, that bet still had a decade of fat on it. In twenty twenty-three, BuzzFeed News had already died on the same weather. The Associated Press wrote that critics called The Messenger's model outdated: social media and search to attract the eyeballs. The Messenger hired into that weather anyway.

Would you have staffed five hundred fifty journalists to chase a hundred million readers Google did not owe you?

Be honest. The poster was beautiful. Centrist. National. Free. The Times had Finkelstein saying the audience would fall in love with media again.

What showed up was smaller. TheWrap, citing the Times, said Comscore put unique visitors around eleven million in October twenty twenty-three. The company told investors December had hit twenty four million. That is their number, not Comscore's. Either way it is not a hundred million. Near the end of twenty twenty-three, revenue was about three million dollars. The Times, via TheWrap, said they had burned more than thirty eight million, and had about one point eight million dollars of cash. The twenty twenty-four dream was still a hundred million of revenue. The Times called that a major turn in fortune.

Three million dollars of ads does not pay three hundred people at prestige rates, plus the leases, plus the travel. You can cut two dozen jobs in January. You cannot turn a three-million-dollar site into a hundred-million-dollar site in a quarter unless the platforms send you a country. They did not.

January second, twenty twenty-four, the Times reported about two dozen layoffs. Richard Beckman, the president, said he was leaving for health. Semafor wrote that the board was looking at a shutdown because the money would be gone by the end of the month. The company said new investment was coming. CNBC, on January tenth, said they were betting on a sudden advertising turnaround, and still talking about Messenger TV.

That is not a strategy. That is a newsroom waiting for a check.

On January thirty-first, Axios and the Times reported the raise had failed. Some staffers, the press said, saw it on the Times and Semafor before they saw it in their own building. Finkelstein emailed anyway. Variety printed the memo. He said they had exhausted every option, literally until earlier that day, and that was why nobody had been told. He cited economic headwinds. He said he was sorry. Press reported the editor in chief, Dan Wakeford, told the newsroom he was not in the loop. The site was wiped to a name and an email address. Last paychecks. Vacation days. Variety and other outlets reported no severance. A national-security reporter posted that healthcare was about to stop.

The next day, former employees sued. That suit is real. It is not this story's ending. This story ends when the ads could not carry the payroll.

Now we open the email again.

The Messenger did not die because journalism died. Journalism was still filing. It died because the unit of the company was a giant newsroom, and the unit of the revenue was leftover display ads. They collected about three million and spent like a paper that already had a century of readers. They optimized for the one metric that made the hole bigger: headcount.

That is the villain. Not a conspiracy. A cost.

You can blame the calendar. You can blame Google. You can feel for the people who showed up on May fifteenth and were gone by January. All of that is real. It is not the mechanism. The mechanism is a two-thousand-ten payroll pointed at a twenty twenty-three ad stack.

If a business only works when somebody else's platform sends a hundred million readers, it is not a newsroom. It is a cost waiting for a miracle. Remember that when the next app hires a city to chase a feed.

This was The Messenger. West Palm Beach. May twenty twenty-three. Fifty million in. About three million of revenue. Dark on January thirty-first, twenty twenty-four. The New York Times called it one of the biggest busts in online news. Public record.
